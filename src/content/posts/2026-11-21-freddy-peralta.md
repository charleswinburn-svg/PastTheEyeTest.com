Peralta, 30, is hitting the free agent market after one of the more intriguing seasons a pitcher has ever had. His 1st half, 2nd half, and postseason all built on each other from a results standpoint, which has led to crowdsourcing being all over the place in terms of what his free agent contract will be.

Peralta’s 2026 1st half, or stint with the Mets in general, was rather underwhelming. Traded for Brandon Sproat and Jett Williams, the Mets hoped to acquire a frontline starter in Freddy Peralta, who finished 5th in 2025 NL Cy Young voting. What they got in his 113.2 innings with the team was anything but, as he finished with a 4.99 ERA in his Mets tenure and was a large reason why they found themselves in the cellar of the NL East.

Peralta was dealt at the deadline to the surging Tampa Bay Rays for relatively cheap at the trade deadline as a rental, where he flourished. In 55.1 innings with the team, he provided a steady presence at the top of the rotation with Drew Rasmussen, finishing with a 3.25 ERA in 55.1 innings, as the Rays stormed to an AL East title and the #1 seed in the AL. In the playoffs, Peralta was a different beast. He pitched to a sub-3 ERA in the postseason and was even named ALCS MVP. The Rays would go on to lose in the World Series, but after what looked like a lost season for Peralta, he carved out a great free-agency case with his 2nd half.

Though Peralta’s 2H results were strong, peripheral data would show that he was maybe getting some unsustainable luck. His SIERA stayed consistently in the mid-4s range, his K-BB% stayed constant, and he was able to reap the benefits of a well-above-average Rays team defense vs. the poor 1H Mets team defense. This would seem to suggest that he just fell into the right situation, with no tangible improvements.

Now let’s get into the pitch data, specifically the change in the pitch data, and how Tampa Bay maximized Peralta. Here, I have his summary with the Mets and with the Rays. Here are some changes I noticed, in order of impact:

- **Release.** Freddy’s one outlier trait is the angles he can produce from his very horizontal release, paired with his flat 4-Seam at the top of the zone. The Rays wanted to exaggerate this, so they moved him toward the 3B side of the rubber and had him extend more down the mound.

Peralta with NYM: 34° arm angle, 5.1’ release height, 3.1’ toward the 3B side, 6.5’ extension<br />
Peralta with TBR: 33° arm angle, 5.1’ release height, **3.4’ toward the 3B side, 6.7’ extension**

- **Usage.** The 2026 Rays became famous for their changeup usage, and Peralta followed suit. Peralta scrapped his gyro slider that he threw with NYM, and in turn threw more changeups. His changeup usage jumped from 20.8% with NYM to 29.7% with TBR. He threw it 10% more to LHH (24% → 34%) and 6% more to RHH (16% → 22%). He also got more whiffs on the offering, jumping from 28.4% to 30.4%.
- **More strikes.** The Rays were also known in 2026 for not walking batters and throwing lots of strikes, which Peralta followed in his time there. Though his strikeouts dropped, he threw 62.9% strikes with TBR compared to just 60.4% with NYM, and in turn improved his BB% from a mediocre 9.4% to a strong 6.9%. The zone rates did go slightly down on his 4-Seam and changeup, but he used his curveball frequently as a called-strike offering, and was able to get outs in chase counts with the 4S-CH combo. His location+ jumped from 95 to 101.

While the peripherals may have stagnated, it’s clear that Peralta was a “better” pitcher in the 2nd half by modeling more indicative of future performance when discussing just his pitches and not the outcomes. My notion is that teams will look at the tangible adjustments that Peralta and the Rays made, and view that as the Freddy Peralta they will get rather than the iffy peripherals and poor 1st half performance. I think Peralta will have a decent market and will end up signing a deal in the 3-4 year, $70-90 million dollar range and give a team some stability as an SP2 or SP3.
